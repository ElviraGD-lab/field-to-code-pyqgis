title: "Prologue: When the lights go out"
---

## 28 April 2025

At 12:33, Spain and Portugal lost power. The whole peninsula, all at once.

Trains and metros stopped. Traffic lights went dark, and cash machines and tills stopped working. Within a few hours, phones began to run flat, the backup batteries at mobile masts ran out, and the signal went with them.

**Electricity isn't a convenience; it's the environment we live in.** Like air, we only notice it when it's gone.

For most people it was all over by nightfall. But for some, a day like that comes round every winter. For them it isn't a whole country that goes dark, just one valley. The power comes back not by evening but after one to three days, and the road that help needs to take is blocked by fallen trees and snow the whole time. That day, for a few hours, we were all in their shoes.

Almost a year later, ENTSO-E, the association of Europe's transmission system operators, published its final analysis. It wasn't a storm, a fire or a tree. It was a cascade of overvoltages: the system couldn't hold its voltage, generators began tripping one after another, and within seconds the whole thing fell apart.[^1]

**A blackout like that is rare.** It happened under an almost clear sky and hit everyone at once. An ordinary outage works differently: it arrives with the weather, starts in one place and lasts longer. And for the people it affects, it's more dangerous.

This book is about the ordinary outage.

## Asturias

**February 2018.** A snowstorm. Trees come down on the lines and hillsides slip. Around 10,000 customers are without power:

- mostly EDP customers in Ibias, Tineo, Somiedo, Cangas del Narcea, Quirós and Laviana;
- around 2,500 Viesgo customers in Mieres, Aller and Lena;
- a few hundred in Los Oscos and Grandas de Salime.[^2]

The most important part of the 112 emergency service's statement is a single sentence. The companies explain why repairs are slow. It isn't down to the crews or to how complex the faults are; it's that the roads and mountain passes are closed by snow. **To repair a line, we first have to reach it.**

**October 2018.** Wet snow, while the trees are still in leaf – the moment they carry the most snow. At the peak, around 15,000 homes are without power, and by evening around 13,500 still are. The list of municipalities is almost the same: Degaña, Ibias, Somiedo, Cangas del Narcea, Quirós, Teverga, Caso, Lena, Aller and Los Oscos. There are 169 incidents in all, and fallen trees block roads in 17 municipalities.[^3]

Put the two reports side by side and the picture is the same:

1. **Trees fall on the lines**, and the power goes.
2. **Trees fall on the roads**, and help can't get through.

And it happens in the same mountain municipalities.

The big lines take a hit too. In March 2011, near the Pajares Pass, on a stretch of line at around 1,800 metres, ice brought down a conductor on the 400 kV Soto–La Robla line, one of the main links between Asturias and León. The line had to be switched off.[^4]

## Spain

The Ministry for the Ecological Transition tracks an indicator called TIEPI. In short, it shows how many hours a year, on average, the capacity connected to the network goes without supply.

**In dispersed rural areas, it rose more than seventyfold in two years.** This is outage time from force majeure:

- 2008: 0.05 hours, or three minutes in the whole year;
- 2009: 1.83 hours;
- 2010: 3.66 hours.[^5]

These are exactly the years of Storm Klaus (January 2009) and Storm Xynthia (February 2010) in northern Spain.

What's more, that's the average across every rural area in the country. Almost four hours on average sounds bearable. For a single mountain village, it can mean several days without power.

In January 2021, Storm Filomena buried the centre of the country in snow. Between 8 and 12 January there were almost 15,000 outages.[^6] In Castile-La Mancha alone, around 27,000 customers lost power, 18,500 of them in the province of Toledo.[^7]

## Europe: three storms and what came out of them

1. **Sweden, 2005, Storm Gudrun.** Around 250 million trees blown down and around 700,000 people without electricity, some of them for up to 35 days. Five weeks without power in a Scandinavian winter.[^8] **What changed:** companies now pay customers compensation for long outages, and a large part of the distribution network has been put underground.

2. **Great Britain, 2021, Storm Arwen.** More than a million customers lost power, around 40,000 of them for more than three days and almost 4,000 for more than a week. Nearly all the damage came from trees and branches. **What the regulator, Ofgem, found:** the money for tree work went on maintaining safety clearances, not on making the network withstand a storm. And the companies didn't know in advance where their most vulnerable customers lived. Compensation came to around £40 million.[^9]

3. **Portugal, 2026, Storm Kristin.** Gusts of around 200 km/h, more than a million customers without power and over 6,000 km of network damaged. Two weeks later, around 30,000 people were still without electricity. **What it cost:** repairing the network cost the company E-REDES around €110 million. That's roughly a fiftieth of the total damage, which was put at €5.3 billion. Only 26% of it was insured.[^10]

And one more figure, from the European Environment Agency: roughly 6 in 10 power failures set off disruption in other sectors, most often telecommunications.[^10] **The lights go out, and soon the phone we'd use to call for help goes quiet too.**

## Why not put it all underground

It's the first thing almost everyone asks. The short answer: underground costs more, is more complicated and takes longer to repair.

1. **It costs more.** For 400 kV lines, underground cable costs €4–8 million per kilometre, and more than €10 million on difficult terrain. An overhead line costs €0.6–1 million in the countryside and €1.5–2 million in the mountains.[^11] In the countryside, one kilometre underground costs as much as 4–13 kilometres on poles and pylons.

2. **It's more complicated.** A long AC cable behaves like a huge capacitor. Beyond roughly 30–35 km it needs reactive power compensation – separate, expensive equipment whose only job is to make the cable work at all.[^11]

3. **It takes longer to repair.** A broken overhead conductor can be seen from the road, and once crews can reach it, it's fixed in hours. A damaged cable has to be located and dug up, and a new section installed, which takes days or weeks.[^11]

Asturias adds its terrain to all this: steep slopes, rock and narrow valleys that lines follow for tens of kilometres. Part of the network can go underground, as it did in Sweden. But most of it will stay on poles and pylons for a very long time.

**So the question isn't how to get rid of overhead lines; it's how to live alongside them and know where the risk lies.**

## How a line endangers the people near it

There are two different things here, and they're easy to confuse.

1. **The danger from the line itself.** A fallen conductor, or one that touches a tree, can set a forest alight. A live conductor on the ground can kill. Both get more dangerous the closer the house is: a house 20 metres from a line and a house a kilometre away are different stories. The consequences can be costly:
   - in California, wildfires started by power lines have become the central theme of utilities' plans;
   - in Chile, a court ordered a company to pay compensation to the victims of a fire that started on a poorly maintained line;[^12]
   - in Spain, one company faced a €10 million bill for a fire started by its line.[^13]

2. **Who loses power.** This doesn't depend on distance from the line. It's decided by the network layout: who sits 'downstream' of the fault. A house right under the line may keep its power while a village three kilometres away loses it.

**This book is about the first:** who lives close to the source of danger and how quickly help can reach it. The second needs the network layout, and that isn't publicly available.

And one thing to clear up straight away: when people in Spain talk about 'homes near high-voltage lines', they usually mean electromagnetic fields and health. This book doesn't deal with that.

## Why it pays the companies themselves

After every major outage, the same question comes up: could it have been foreseen?

Spain's regulation for high-voltage overhead lines, ITC-LAT 07, already requires checks on trees that could fall onto a conductor. But the check is purely geometric: can the tree reach the conductor or not?[^14] The regulation doesn't say:

- where people live nearby;
- which of them are older and more vulnerable;
- how many minutes it takes the fire service to reach them;
- which stretch to inspect first.

**That's the difference between complying with the regulation and managing risk.** If a company knows in advance where its lines run closest to homes and where help takes longest to arrive, it starts clearance on those stretches and warns the emergency services ahead of time. And it can show that the risk is known and being dealt with. That's cheaper than compensation, court cases and lost trust.

The law is heading the same way. Spain is preparing a law on the protection and resilience of critical entities, transposing EU Directive 2022/2557. Energy facilities designated as critical will have to assess their risks, natural hazards included, and have a resilience plan. A missing plan, or one with serious shortcomings, can bring a fine of up to €10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover.[^15]

## What already exists

1. **The United States.** Utilities write wildfire mitigation plans and calculate risk as likelihood multiplied by consequence. Consequence is modelled from building density and vegetation on a grid of roughly 200-metre cells.[^16] But the models are closed, commercial and built for Californian wildfires.

2. **Asturias.** In 2024, Red Eléctrica and the regional government of the Principality agreed to de-energise lines during fires, inspect line routes in areas with frequent fires, and share the fire danger index.[^17] The new wildfire plan for 2026–2030 calculates how long it takes to reach a fire in each district.[^18] But it works from past fires, not from homes near lines.

We looked for an open tool that, for any municipality, answers both questions at once: which homes stand close to overhead lines, and how long help takes to reach them by day and by night. **We found no open tool of that kind.**

## What we'll build

So we'll build it ourselves, from scratch, in QGIS and Python.

The input is the name of a municipality. The output:

- residential buildings grouped by distance band from the lines;
- how many dwellings they contain and when they were built;
- how many minutes the fire service needs to reach each building by day and by night;
- the places where a home is close to a line and far from help;
- a map of where the open data on lines is complete and where it has gaps.

**What the tool doesn't claim:**

- a home within a line's safety clearance zone isn't a breach of the regulation, just a candidate for inspection;
- travel times assume clear roads, so in a real storm they'll be longer, not shorter;
- the tool doesn't assess electromagnetic fields.

We'll build it using the Asturian municipality of Lena as our example. We can take the same route for any municipality, in any country with power lines, homes and roads: we follow the same code and swap in our own data wherever the book says so.

We'll walk the whole route together. We'll see the reasoning, the search for solutions and, of course, the mistakes, because there's no learning without them.

This book isn't about PyQGIS for its own sake. It's about using it to solve a real problem.

We hope that along the way we'll come to love PyQGIS and see the beauty in the logic of code.
